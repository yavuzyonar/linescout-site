How Sports Betting Odds Work: American, Decimal, and Fractional Explained

Published August 20, 2026 · 7 min read · By the OddsLighthouse Editorial Team

Every sportsbook shows odds in one of three formats: American, decimal, or fractional. They all describe the exact same thing — how much you can win, and how likely the book thinks an outcome is — just written differently. Once you can read one format, the others take about five minutes to learn.

American odds (the US standard)

American odds are written as a number with a plus or minus sign, always relative to a $100 bet.

  • Negative numbers (e.g., -150): how much you'd need to bet to win $100. A -150 favorite means betting $150 wins you $100 profit (plus your $150 back).
  • Positive numbers (e.g., +130): how much you'd win on a $100 bet. A +130 underdog means betting $100 wins you $130 profit (plus your $100 back).

You don't have to bet exactly $100 — the ratio scales. A $20 bet at +130 wins $26 profit; a $20 bet at -150 wins $13.33 profit.

Decimal odds (common outside the US)

Decimal odds show your total payout — stake included — per $1 wagered. A decimal of 2.30 means a $10 bet returns $23 total ($13 profit plus your original $10). Decimal odds are popular because the math is simple: payout = stake × decimal odds.

Fractional odds (common in the UK, especially horse racing)

Fractional odds like 5/2 mean you win $5 for every $2 wagered (plus your stake back). A $20 bet at 5/2 wins $50 profit.

What odds tell you about probability

Odds aren't just about payout size — they encode the sportsbook's estimate of how likely an outcome is. This is called implied probability. For American odds:

  • Favorite (negative): implied probability = odds ÷ (odds + 100), e.g. -150 → 150/250 = 60%
  • Underdog (positive): implied probability = 100 ÷ (odds + 100), e.g. +130 → 100/230 ≈ 43.5%

Notice those two numbers add up to more than 100% (60% + 43.5% = 103.5%). That extra 3.5% is the sportsbook's built-in margin, often called the vig or juice — it's how books make money regardless of who wins. Recognizing the vig is the first step toward evaluating whether a bet is actually good value, not just whether a team is likely to win.

Takeaway: Odds are a price, not a prediction of certainty. A -150 favorite still loses roughly 2 out of every 5 times by the book's own math — factor that into how much of your bankroll you're willing to risk on any single bet.
