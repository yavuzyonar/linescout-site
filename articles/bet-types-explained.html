Moneylines, Spreads, Parlays, and Props: Every Common Bet Type Explained

Published August 20, 2026 · 9 min read · By the OddsLighthouse Editorial Team

Open any sportsbook app and you'll see a dozen ways to bet on the same game. Here's what each one actually means, in plain terms, with an example.

Moneyline — just pick the winner

The simplest bet: pick who wins, straight up, no point spread involved. Odds are higher on underdogs and lower on favorites (see our guide to odds). Example: Team A -180 vs. Team B +150 — betting Team A wins less per dollar because they're favored to win outright.

Point spread — betting on the margin

The spread handicaps the favorite so both sides are closer to an even-money bet. Example: Team A -6.5 means Team A must win by 7+ points for that bet to pay; betting Team B +6.5 pays out if Team B wins outright or loses by 6 or fewer points. The ".5" exists specifically to avoid ties ("pushes").

Totals (over/under) — betting on combined score

Instead of picking a side, you bet whether the combined score of both teams will go over or under a number set by the book, e.g. Over/Under 47.5 points.

Parlays — combining multiple bets into one

A parlay links two or more bets together; all of them have to win for the parlay to pay out, but the payout multiplies with each added leg. A 3-leg parlay at roughly -110 odds each pays out around 6-to-1 — much higher than any single bet — but the probability of hitting all three drops fast. Sportsbooks make a larger built-in margin on parlays than on single bets, which is exactly why they're promoted so heavily.

Parlays are the highest-variance common bet type. A single missed leg loses the entire parlay, no matter how well the other legs did. Treat parlay stakes as a small, separate part of your overall bankroll plan — see our bankroll management guide.

Player and team props — betting on specific stats, not the final result

A "prop" (proposition bet) is a wager on something other than the final score — a player's total points, whether a specific player scores first, or a team's total rebounds. Props are popular because they let you bet on a specific favorite player rather than the game outcome.

Futures — betting on an outcome weeks or months away

Futures are long-term bets, like which team wins the championship before the season starts. Your money is tied up until the outcome is settled, so futures should be sized differently than single-game bets — factor the tied-up time into your bankroll planning.

Live (in-play) betting — betting while the game is happening

Odds update in real time as the game unfolds, letting you bet on what happens next (next score, next quarter's total, etc.). It's fast-paced and easy to bet impulsively — the same bankroll rules apply, arguably more strictly, since decisions happen in seconds rather than with time to think.

Takeaway: Every bet type is a different way of packaging the same underlying odds and vig. None of them "beat the house" by default — understanding what you're actually wagering on is what lets you make an informed choice.
